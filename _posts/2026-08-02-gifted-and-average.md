There is a moment in *The Princess Bride* when the grandson interrupts the story to point out that he had predicted the plot correctly. His grandfather answers: "Yes, you're very smart. Shut up."

When I was raising my children, I referenced this quote frequently.

It makes me laugh. But I also hear something unexpectedly healthy in it.

He is not telling the child to hide his intelligence, pretend to be less capable, or make himself smaller so other people feel comfortable. He is saying, affectionately: *We know. Your intelligence is already established. But that's not the most important thing right now.*

Yes, you are very smart.

Now there is a whole person to develop.

## The pressure to live up to potential

Children identified as [gifted](/blog/giftedness/) may absorb more than simple information about how their minds work.

They may also hear:

- You have enormous potential.
- You are destined to do something important.
- You should be able to excel without much help.
- Your adult life ought to be as exceptional as your test scores.
- It would be a terrible waste not to make full use of your abilities.

The message is well intended. Adults see real capacity and want to nurture it.

But "potential" can become less like possibility and more like an obligation.

A gifted child may grow into an adult who feels responsible for producing a life impressive enough to justify the early promise. An ordinary career can feel disappointing. A half-finished book, an abandoned degree, or a season spent caring for children may become evidence of failure. Even a good life may not feel good enough if it does not look proportionately amazing.

How extraordinary should your life be if your childhood IQ was in the 98th percentile? What achievement would finally prove you did not waste it?

There is no way to answer that. The question itself is the problem.

## Most of us are average at most things

In his essay ["In Defense of Being Average"](https://markmanson.net/being-average), Mark Manson argues for accepting mediocrity when it is the outcome of our efforts—not making mediocrity the goal, but recognizing that most of us will land somewhere near the middle in most areas of life.

That remains true when someone is unusually intelligent.

A gifted person may still be an average athlete, cook, musician, parent, conversationalist, gardener, dancer, or romantic partner. They may be below average at keeping track of paperwork, remembering names, loading the dishwasher efficiently, or knowing what to say at a party.

Even in intellectual life, giftedness does not make someone equally capable in every subject. A person may understand complex abstractions and struggle to learn a language. They may produce brilliant ideas and be an entirely ordinary writer. They may be unusually perceptive and still be wrong—sometimes spectacularly wrong.

None of this cancels out giftedness. It simply places giftedness inside a whole human life.

## The limits of being smart

I think of two very different messages a smart child might receive.

One child learns:

> You are so smart that you should not have to work.

Tasks that require repetition are treated as beneath them. Natural ability is praised; effort is unnecessary. When something finally becomes difficult, the child has little experience tolerating frustration, changing strategies, or remaining engaged while feeling incompetent.

Another child learns:

> Yes, you are smart. That is one important thing about you. Now we are also going to help you develop physically, socially, emotionally, and practically.

This child may be encouraged to try activities that do not come naturally. They learn to throw a ball even when they are bad at it. They go camping even if they would rather stay home on their computer. They are expected to do chores, build relationships, contribute to their home and community, and gradually become more independent.

Their intelligence is acknowledged securely enough that it does not need to exempt them from ordinary human development.

Carol Dweck's research points at something similar. In a 1998 study with Claudia Mueller, fifth graders were praised either for being smart or for working hard. The children praised for intelligence went on to prefer easier problems, and after a round of failure they persisted less, enjoyed the work less, performed worse, and were more likely to conclude that they simply lacked ability. They also described intelligence as a fixed trait rather than something that could grow.

Mindset research has had a mixed record since—later attempts to reproduce the praise effect have not always succeeded, and classroom mindset interventions have generally produced smaller results than the early enthusiasm promised. But the core observation matches what I see constantly. When being smart is the thing you are praised for, difficulty starts to feel like disconfirming evidence.

Real life requires capacities that intelligence cannot substitute for:

- showing up consistently
- tolerating discomfort
- caring for a body
- maintaining relationships
- doing repetitive tasks
- asking for help
- practicing something badly before doing it well
- continuing when insight has not produced immediate change

Understanding what needs to be done is not the same as being able or willing to do it.

## When failure threatens an identity

For someone whose identity formed around being smart, effort can feel surprisingly dangerous.

If you are smart, shouldn't this be easier?

If you need instruction, were you overrated?

If you try sincerely and fail, what will be left of the version of you who was supposed to be exceptional?

I think of a talented artist who illustrated a children's book and could not enjoy the accomplishment, because she was afraid the finished work would not be amazing. But art does not come with an objective percentile ranking. What if illustrating a children's book were allowed to be simply interesting, creative, and deeply worthwhile? What might become possible if the work did not have to justify the artist identity?

Avoidance can protect the identity. You cannot discover that you are an average musician if you never take lessons. You cannot be rejected on a date if you never ask anyone out. You cannot write a mediocre first draft if you keep researching the book instead of beginning it.

This is [perfectionism](/2026-08-16-trauma-neurodivergence-why-not-both/), though it does not always look like the tidy, high-achieving version. Sometimes perfectionism looks like a person who never starts. Gifted underachievement is often described as a motivation problem. It may be closer to a protection problem.

The cost is that protecting the identity of being gifted can prevent the person from building a life.

Accepting averageness makes room for **beginner's mind**: the willingness to encounter something without already knowing how to do it, and without requiring immediate competence as proof that you belong there.

You can take a dance class and stand on the wrong foot. You can learn to date in middle age and discover that much of it feels unnatural. You can make awkward attempts at emotional honesty. You can begin strength training with unimpressive weights. You can create something that does not live up to the version you imagined.

A teacher of gifted children once told me that the most important thing she taught her students was how to fail. For many gifted kids, an imperfect score on a test or assignment is not a routine part of learning but a dysregulating tragedy. Fear of failure keeps them from the experiences that would teach them they can survive it—and many of the best experiences in life are on the other side of that.

Being bad at a new skill is not evidence about your worth or intelligence. It is the ordinary starting point. As Jake the Dog says in *Adventure Time*, "Dude, sucking at something is the first step to being sorta good at something."

## You do not have to keep proving it

Some gifted adults also feel a continuing need for other people to recognize their intelligence.

Being misunderstood may feel intolerable. A correction may become a contest. A conversation may become an opportunity to establish expertise. The person may feel diminished when their contribution goes unnoticed or when someone else is more knowledgeable.

This makes sense if being smart became an important source of safety, belonging, or identity.

But it is exhausting to depend on every room reflecting you correctly.

A more secure relationship with giftedness sounds like:

> My intelligence is real whether or not this person sees it, whether or not it is useful here, and whether or not I am currently succeeding.

You do not have to insert your full résumé into every conversation. You do not have to win every disagreement. You do not have to demonstrate all the layers you noticed.

Sometimes your intelligence matters enormously. Sometimes another person's patience, practical knowledge, kindness, courage, or experience matters more.

Either way, you are still you.

## What do you actually want your life to be?

Releasing the demand to "live up to your potential" does not mean abandoning ambition.

It means ambition can become more personal and less performative.

Instead of asking:

> What life would be impressive enough for someone with my abilities?

you can ask:

- What holds my attention?
- What kind of work feels meaningful?
- Where do my interests and abilities meet something other people need?
- What [relationships](/2026-08-09-gifted-loneliness/) make me more alive?
- What am I willing to practice, even when I am not immediately good at it?
- What kind of ordinary days would I be glad to live repeatedly?

Your answers may lead to something conventionally extraordinary.

Or they may lead to satisfying work, a few deep relationships, creative projects no one else sees, service to a small community, a garden, an absorbing hobby, or a home filled with books and people you love.

The value of the life does not depend on how unusual or impressive it looks from the outside.

## Permission to be fully human

Giftedness matters. It can shape how you learn, connect, work, and experience the world. Denying it may require its own kind of self-erasure.

But giftedness is not a mandate to become extraordinary in every dimension—or to build a life that proves the assessment was correct.

You are allowed to be remarkably capable in some ways and thoroughly average in most others.

Yes, you are very smart.

Now you are free to become the rest of yourself.
