When people imagine emotional dysregulation, they often picture something dramatic: crying, yelling, panic, being impulsive, or losing control. But sometimes dysregulation looks unusually calm.

A person may become more articulate, precise, analytical, and certain. They may speak in an even voice while carefully explaining why their interpretation is the only reasonable one. They may appear to be the most rational person in the room. And they may have very little access to what is happening emotionally inside them.

For [gifted adults](/blog/giftedness/), intelligence can make this state especially difficult to recognize. The mind is still working, sometimes impressively. But it is working with incomplete information.

I think of this as becoming **rigidly intelligent**.

## When intelligence loses flexibility

Intelligence is not only the ability to construct a strong argument. It also includes curiosity, perspective-taking, updating our conclusions, recognizing uncertainty, and considering information that does not fit our first interpretation.

Under stress, some of those capacities can become less available.

A person may remain highly verbal and logical while becoming:

- unable to recognize fear, hurt, shame, or attachment threat underneath the analysis
- focused on proving a case rather than understanding what happened
- suspicious of perspectives they would ordinarily consider

Their intelligence has been recruited to protect them. The mind becomes an excellent attorney for one interpretation rather than a curious investigator of several possibilities.

## The emotion is still there

Emotions are still there when you are hyperlogical. But it may mean the emotion has become difficult to identify directly. Fear can become risk analysis. Hurt can become criticism. Shame can become defensiveness. Attachment threat can become a detailed explanation of why the other person is no longer trustworthy.

The person may sincerely believe they are setting emotion aside and looking only at the facts. But unrecognized emotion is still influencing which facts seem important, which memories come to mind, and what conclusions feel obvious.

This is one reason emotional disconnection can paradoxically make a person **less** rational. They are attempting to understand reality while excluding a major source of information about their own experience.

## Calm is not always regulation

Some people equate regulation with not raising their voice. But calm behavior and nervous-system regulation are not necessarily the same thing.

John and Julie Gottman spent years watching couples argue while wired to sensors in their “Love Lab.” They found that at a certain level of physiological arousal—racing heart, shallow breathing, what they called **flooding**—people became unable to listen, take in new information, or problem-solve. 

The partner who withdrew and went quiet was just as flooded as the partner who raised their voice. They could describe the argument in detail. They could not tell you that their body was in an overwhelmed state. The sensors knew something the person didn’t.

A person may look composed while becoming emotionally unreachable. They may withdraw into analysis, stop trusting the person in front of them, and lose access to warmth, playfulness, curiosity, or care.

From the outside, their partner or friend may experience them as cold, rigid, or impossible to reach. From the inside, they may feel clearheaded and convinced that they are simply responding to reality.

This can be especially painful in close relationships. Repair isn’t about who has the strongest argument. It requires access to the emotional experience underneath the conflict:

- I feel threatened.
- I am afraid you will leave.
- I feel embarrassed.
- I wanted you to understand me.
- I am hurt, and I do not yet know what to do with that.

Simple words like these can be the brave first steps back into connection to emotions and connection.

## Is this pattern gendered?

This pattern can occur in anyone. It may, however, be especially familiar to men.

Many boys are encouraged to develop competence, control, independence, and logical problem-solving while receiving much less practice identifying and communicating vulnerable emotions. Fear, sadness, shame, dependency, and longing are discouraged or treated as weakness.

The emotions are still there though. They may instead emerge as withdrawal, certainty, criticism, anger, or relentless problem-solving.

Gottmans’ research supports this. He found that men tended to flood faster; it took less conflict for their bodies to register threat.

This can create the familiar claim that one person is "logical" while someone else is "emotional." In reality, both people are having emotions. One person may simply have more practice recognizing theirs.

Giftedness can strengthen this protective pattern because the argument may be genuinely sophisticated. A person can construct a highly coherent explanation while remaining unaware that their perspective has narrowed.

Of course this is not just male. Women whose [safety, identity, or belonging has depended on competence](/blog/hyper-independence/) can develop the same style. 

## The cost of being cut off

When people have limited access to their emotional experience, they may also have less access to what they want, what they need, when a boundary has been crossed, when an old injury is shaping a current reaction, and the vulnerability that creates closeness.

Relationships can become less intimate because others interact with the logic layer rather than the feelings underneath them. This can also lead to lower quality decision-making. Someone may end a relationship, reject feedback, leave a job, or form a lasting judgment based on their emotional experience in the moment.

## How to recognize rigid intelligence

It can be really difficult to recognize being in this state.

If you usually have good access to your emotions, the loss of that access is itself a signal. Something has changed, and the change is information.

But many of my clients [struggle to notice their emotions or their bodily experience](/blog/gifted-intellectualizing-feelings/), and that makes it tricky to know that this is happening. With practice, even people disconnected from body and emotions might notice subtle cues such as:

- a sudden increase in certainty
- mentally rehearsing the same argument, or needing to rant
- explaining another person's motives as though they are established facts
- losing curiosity about their perspective
- feeling compelled to resolve the issue immediately
- becoming calm but emotionally distant
- believing that your lack of visible emotion proves your objectivity
- building increasingly elaborate evidence for one conclusion
- someone telling you that you have gone cold

A useful question is:

> Am I becoming more thoughtful, or simply more certain?

Another is:

> What emotion would make sense underneath this analysis?

You don’t have to know the exact answer. Even saying, "Something in me feels threatened right now," can create an important degree of separation between your current state and the story it is producing.

And often self-monitoring is not enough. It may take another person to notice: perhaps a partner who says you have become unreachable, or a therapist watching it happen in the room. 

## Pause before reaching a verdict

When you notice this pattern, the goal is to postpone conclusions until more of your experience is available.

A brief sequence might look like this:

1. **Name the state.** "I am activated." "I feel defensive." "Something here feels threatening."
2. **Pause major conclusions.** Avoid deciding what another person intended, what the relationship means, or what permanent action is required while your frame is narrow.
3. **Notice the body.** Is there pressure in the chest, tension in the jaw, heaviness, heat, numbness, or an urge to withdraw or attack?
4. **Identify the vulnerable emotion if you can.** Fear, hurt, shame, sadness, disappointment, or longing may be underneath the certainty.
5. **Allow time, and use time correctly.** Gottman found that arousal drops back toward baseline in roughly twenty minutes, but only if the person actually disengages. If you spend the break rehearsing your argument and assembling evidence, the physiology does not settle. Sleep, movement, quiet, or a genuinely different task works. Continuing the fight in your head does not.
6. **Return with curiosity.** What did you actually observe? What did you assume? What else could explain it?

The aim is to bring reason back after emotion has been recognized, so intelligence can work with a fuller set of information.

## What therapy can offer

A therapist can notice what a sensor would: the moment a client becomes more articulate but less connected, more certain but less curious.

That outside view matters most for the people who cannot feel the shift themselves. Someone has to be tracking the thing they cannot track.

Over time, the person may become able to pause and say:

> I know I have a compelling explanation right now. I also know that I feel threatened, and I may not have the whole picture yet.

That is intelligence becoming flexible again.
