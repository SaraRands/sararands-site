*This post is adapted from the first half of a [talk I gave at Salt Lake Oasis](/speaking/) on October 26, 2025.*

Why is it so hard to change?

Humans have been asking themselves this since there were humans, in all sorts of contexts: religion, philosophy, our relationships with others, our relationship with ourselves. We intend to change, and then we keep doing the thing we always have done. Or we desperately want someone else to change. Either way, we feel terrible.

Modern neuroscience gives us some new answers.

Some nuances before I start: I’m not a neuroscientist. I’m a therapist. I dabble in neuroscience towards the goal of helping people change. Sometimes we use models that are simplified towards the goal of helping people reach their goals.

## Our brain’s wiring

Our brain’s wiring is one reason why change is so difficult.

Brains use a lot of energy. About 20% of our calories get spent running this small organ that’s just 2% of our body weight.

Because thinking is so expensive, our brains evolved to be efficient. Brains conserve energy by maintaing old patterns.

Our brain lays down tracks in childhood. In this way, our parents’ stuff and previous generations’ stuff gets passed down to us.

And like old cow trails eventually become roads even if no modern person would choose that path for the road, the brain keeps going down that path of least resistance. That makes those tracks get deeper and deeper over time, like a small trickle of water that becomes a deep riverbed. 

But what if your brain’s wiring could change? Imagine an ocean wave that deposits sand that smooths out the grooves in the sand, enabling new, more adaptive paths to form. Or, as one of my colleagues once put it, shaking the Etch-a-Sketch. 

This is called **neuroplasticity**: our brain’s ability to change and adapt from experience.

Scientists used to think that after a certain age the brain stopped changing. It turns out that’s not true. One of the gifts of modern neuroscience is the knowledge that **our brain never stops changing in response to learning**. 

**Neuroplasticity is the science of hope.**

Now, neuroplasticity is a double-edged sword that can cut both ways. Our brain’s wiring can also change for the worse, because of things like unintegrated trauma, isolation, addiction, chronic pain, poverty, stress, sleep deprivation, and shame. So keep that in mind.

I’m focusing here on the many ways we can change for the better.

## Many roads

Healing trauma means creating and reinforcing new neural pathways.

Modern brain imaging research shows that changes from certain practices: things like:

* Mindfulness and meditation
* Talk therapy
* Good health habits: sleep, diet, exercise
* Play
* Healthy relationships
* Psychedelics

(Psychedelics is is an exciting up-and-coming field. In the Oasis presentation I did not get into the details because of the legal, medical, and psychological risks. There is a great deal of positive research about the therapeutic benefits, especially when paired with psychotherapy, and my hope is that the legal landscape changes as a result of this reseach.)
