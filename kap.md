# Ketamine-Assisted Psychotherapy (KAP)

*I'm currently exploring options for the medical side of offering KAP in my private practice, including who I'll collaborate with for prescribing, administration, and medical monitoring. I'm not scheduling KAP sessions yet, but I'd love to know if you're interested.*

Ketamine is a dissociative anesthetic that has been used in medicine for decades. At doses below those used for general anesthesia, it can produce an altered (or non-ordinary) state of consciousness. Ketamine-assisted psychotherapy (KAP) makes therapeutic use of this state, with psychological support before, during, and after the medicine experience.

I was an '80s *This Is Your Brain on Drugs* kid. I grew up Mormon, had addiction in my extended family, and considered psychedelics somewhere between hippie and irresponsible. Even after listening to Michael Pollan's *How to Change Your Mind* on a road trip in 2018, I found the research compelling but not relevant to my life.

By the time I finished graduate school in 2024, the clinical conversation around psychedelics had shifted. My first job as a therapist was at a practice that offered ketamine-assisted psychotherapy, and I had the opportunity to experience it myself and subsequently work with clients.

I saw people who had been suicidal become able to contemplate living again. I saw longstanding obsessive patterns loosen. I saw people become able to feel emotions that had previously been too painful to approach. I also saw how ketamine could complement other forms of trauma therapy, sometimes bringing something into awareness that we could work with more deliberately afterward.

I've since completed the [2025–2026 Polaris Insight Center Ketamine-Assisted Psychotherapy Training Cohort](https://training.polarisinsight.com/cohort), assisted with a Polaris experiential training, and am pursuing advanced certification.

I'm excited about what ketamine makes possible in therapy. It's not a miracle drug, it doesn't do the work for you, and it's not for everyone. I think it's worth understanding both what it can do and where its limitations are.

## Why ketamine?

Much of the research on ketamine in mental health has focused on [treatment-resistant depression](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2020.20081251) and [suicidal ideation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28969441/). Unlike conventional antidepressants, which typically take weeks to have an effect, ketamine can reduce depressive symptoms and suicidal thoughts within hours or days. Ketamine is also being studied for [PTSD, OCD, and other mental health conditions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39564613/), though the evidence for these uses is less established.

There are two aspects of ketamine that are interesting from a psychotherapy standpoint.

One is [neuroplasticity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41475562/)—the brain's capacity to change. Ketamine appears to temporarily enhance neuroplasticity, potentially making it easier to develop new patterns of thinking, feeling, and responding. This is one of the reasons for combining ketamine with psychotherapy.

The other is the subjective experience itself. In a non-ordinary state of consciousness, you may experience time, your body, your emotions, your memories, and your sense of who you are much differently than you ordinarily do. Familiar experiences may take on new meanings. You may make associations that would never have occurred to you in your ordinary waking state. [Research on psychedelics](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40031999/) has found that what researchers call *mystical experiences*—experiences of awe, profound connection, unity, or transcendence of time and space—are associated with better therapeutic outcomes. The relationship is less clear for ketamine specifically. You don't need to have spiritual beliefs to have these experiences, nor do you need to have a mystical experience to benefit from ketamine.

In KAP, we make therapeutic use of the medicine and the experience it creates. [Research on this combination](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41650725/) is still developing.

## Who might benefit from KAP?

Some people seek out ketamine because they've been living with depression for years and haven't found adequate relief. Others are interested in what an altered state of consciousness might make possible in the context of ongoing therapy.

You might be curious about KAP if:

- you understand your patterns but are having trouble changing them
- you know the healthy thoughts but don't believe them
- you've worked on self-compassion for years but your inner critic is still going strong
- there are experiences in your past that are difficult to approach in therapy
- you're wrestling with questions of mortality, identity, or meaning

I've noticed that anxious, precise, conscientious, detail-oriented people (i.e. my people) can sometimes benefit from psychedelic experiences. Psychedelics temporarily loosen our usual grip on reality. If your grip tends to be firm already, some loosening can be therapeutic. But you can't do that on command. KAP offers a supportive environment in which to experience that.

Of course, loosening that grip isn't beneficial for everyone. If you already have difficulty staying grounded in reality, an altered state of consciousness can be destabilizing. Vulnerability to psychosis or mania can be a reason not to use ketamine, as can other medical and psychiatric factors.

As a psychotherapist, I can help you explore whether KAP makes sense in the context of your therapeutic work. The medical provider I collaborate with will evaluate whether ketamine is an appropriate and safe medication for you.

## KAP and neurodivergence

Much of my practice is devoted to working with gifted, autistic, ADHD, and highly sensitive adults.

If you've spent much of your life being misunderstood by people whose minds work differently from yours, it matters who you choose to be with you in an altered state of consciousness.

We're still learning about how different kinds of minds experience psychedelics. Our ways of thinking, perceiving, imagining, making associations, and experiencing emotion vary enormously in ordinary waking consciousness. I suspect there's probably even more variation in non-ordinary states.

I want to make room for the way your mind works, both during the medicine experience and as we make sense of it together afterward.

## KAP and trauma therapy

A physical therapist might encourage you to take your pain medication before a session to make the exercises tolerable.

I think about ketamine, itself an anesthetic, in much the same way. I've seen people revisit painful experiences and feel emotions that would ordinarily be too overwhelming to approach. This can allow them to move through the pain they've been carrying and finally let it go.

An altered state of consciousness can also bring things into awareness that aren't readily accessible through conversation. Think about the kinds of material that come up in dreams: images, metaphors, fragments of memories.

Some of my colleagues and I have noticed similarities between the altered-state experience of ketamine and that of [Accelerated Resolution Therapy (ART)](/blog/accelerated-resolution-therapy/). ART can feel a bit like lucid dreaming: you're immersed in imagery but conscious and able to direct the experience. I've found these two modalities complement each other well. Sometimes an image surfaces during a ketamine session that we can return to and explore more deliberately in ART.

## Preparation

Before doing KAP together, I want us to have established some trust. You're going to be under the influence of a drug in my presence, and I want you to feel comfortable asking me for whatever you need.

We'll talk about what ketamine feels like, what you might experience, and anything you're apprehensive about.

One skill that comes in handy during a psychedelic experience is the ability to notice what's happening without immediately trying to change it. If you've practiced mindfulness meditation, you may already have some experience with this. We can also practice these skills in therapy before you try ketamine.

We'll set an intention for your medicine session: what you're hoping to explore or what you'd like some help with. It's important to go into the experience with an intention, but to hold it loosely. The medicine may have its own ideas.

## What happens during a medicine session?

I stay with you throughout the ketamine experience. Part of my job is to be the grown-up in the room. You don't need to keep track of time, manage the music, or monitor what's happening around you. You're free to explore your internal experience.

Some people spend most of the session quietly listening to music. Others want to talk about what they're experiencing. I generally follow your lead.

If you get caught in a distressing thought loop or encounter something frightening, sometimes a question or a playful suggestion from outside the experience can help you find another direction.

One of my trainers encouraged us to approach psychedelic experiences with curiosity: if you encounter a door, open it. If you encounter a body of water, dive in.

Many therapeutic modalities, including [NARM](https://narmtraining.com/what-is-narm/), work with the concept of an *inner healing intelligence*: the idea that something in us moves toward healing, much as a plant turns toward sunlight. I'm interested in seeing where your mind takes you rather than deciding ahead of time where I think you need to go.

Ketamine can also temporarily lower some of your usual psychological defenses. I'm mindful of the vulnerability that creates, and I don't use it as an opportunity to push you into territory you haven't chosen to explore.

Because you're in a vulnerable state while under the influence of ketamine, I plan to require either a second practitioner in the room or video recording of the session. This is to protect both of us. We'll discuss these safeguards, including consent and confidentiality, before your first medicine session.

## Integration

After a medicine session, we'll spend some time making sense of the experience. You may have questions about what happened. We'll explore the images, metaphors, emotions, and ideas that came up, like we might explore a dream. Something can feel profoundly true during a psychedelic experience without it necessarily being true. You remain the authority and interpreter of your experience.

We can also return to any traumatic material that came up during the session and see whether there's some remaining work to do.

I'm interested in what, from a sober place, you want to carry forward into your life. Is there something you want to do differently? Something you want to try?

Ketamine may make it easier to change, but you still have to do something with that opportunity. Consider which habits you'd like to interrupt and which new ones you'd like to practice.

## Expectations and risks

One idea I liked from my Polaris training was to think of ketamine as a co-therapist who will sometimes disappoint you. People vary in how they respond to the medicine itself. You may have an extraordinary experience, or you may spend much of the session wondering when something is going to happen. Sometimes a seemingly unremarkable session turns out to have been useful, and sometimes a profound experience doesn't translate into much change in your life.

There are parallels between psychedelics and meditation, both in their potential benefits and in their risks. Both can change how we experience ourselves and the world, but psychedelics can do this on a much shorter timescale. It's easy to get in over your head, and unlike with meditation, you can't simply decide you're done and walk away.

A difficult psychedelic experience isn't necessarily a bad therapeutic experience. There is value in working through something difficult with someone you trust. But an experience can also be overwhelming or destabilizing. This is why preparation, medical screening and monitoring, therapeutic support during the experience, and integration afterward all matter.

There are many roads to Rome. Psychedelics are just one path to healing. But the road can be a long one if you're dealing with complex trauma or intractable depression, and ketamine may make some of that work easier. And when someone is struggling with suicidal thoughts, the possibility of rapid relief takes on a different significance.

I never want a client to feel pressured to try ketamine.

## Medical safety

Ketamine has a long history of medical use, from battlefield anesthesia in Vietnam to pediatric procedures and emergency medicine. It has a well-established safety profile when used in appropriate medical settings. Its use in KAP is off-label and requires medical screening and monitoring.

Ketamine can temporarily raise blood pressure, cause nausea, and affect your balance and coordination. Even walking to the bathroom may require assistance during a medicine session. More serious complications are possible.

**You must arrange for someone to drive you home after your session. You cannot drive for the remainder of the day, or longer if directed by the medical provider.**

Before beginning KAP, you'll need to be evaluated and medically cleared by the medical provider I collaborate with. They will be responsible for prescribing, dosing, and medical monitoring. My role is the psychotherapy.

As a cancer survivor, I'm interested in what psychedelic therapy can offer people learning to live with the awareness of their own mortality.

Cancer can leave us with both medical trauma and existential questions that aren't easily resolved through thinking. KAP may offer another way to work with both.

## Psychedelic integration

I also work with people who have had psychedelic experiences elsewhere, whether in a therapeutic setting or on their own.

You may have had an experience that changed how you see yourself or the world, and you're trying to figure out what to do with that. Or you may have had a confusing or frightening experience that you're still trying to make sense of.

[Accelerated Resolution Therapy (ART)](/blog/accelerated-resolution-therapy/) can be useful here, too. We can revisit imagery from a psychedelic experience with more conscious direction, explore its meaning, or work to reduce the emotional charge of distressing memories.

Psychedelic integration is available as part of individual therapy. You don't need to be interested in KAP.
